# Top10QB Strategy — 2026 Season

*Rewritten July 2026 · Season 1 operating plan*

---

## 1. The Idea

Top10QB is one person ranking the ten NFL quarterbacks he trusts most right now.

The opinions are real. The overly official presentation is the joke.

The weekly list is the product. X is where people discover it and argue with it. The website is
the permanent record: the current list, the old lists, the reasoning, and the calls that aged
badly.

The goal is not to imitate ESPN with fewer resources. The goal is to feel like the best version of
a smart football conversation with a friend who has watched the games, has a point of view, and is
willing to keep the receipts.

## 2. The Ranking Rule

Every list answers one question:

> **Which ten quarterbacks would I trust most to play a game this Sunday?**

That definition matters. Without it, every disagreement turns into an argument about a different
question.

This is:

- A current ranking, with enough history to avoid overreacting to one quarter
- A judgment about quarterback play, not simply team wins
- A real opinion, not a formula pretending to remove opinion

This is not:

- A career résumé
- An MVP ballot
- A dynasty or fantasy ranking
- A prediction of who will have the best statistics next week

When the list changes, the explanation should usually be about new evidence, not a new definition.

## 3. Positioning and Voice

### The useful tension

Top10QB should sound confident enough to be worth arguing with and self-aware enough to be fun.

The brand works when both sides are present:

- **Confidence:** “Stafford is third. The throws are still absurd.”
- **Self-awareness:** “I have no vote, no sources, and no reason for the graphic to look this
  official.”

If it is only confident, the account becomes another angry sports account. If it is only
self-deprecating, nobody has a reason to care about the ranking.

### Voice rules

1. Write in the first person most of the time. “I” sounds more human than referring to “the
   Rankmaster” in every post.
2. Use the Rankmaster and “World Renowned List” as recurring bits, not as a costume that has to
   appear in every sentence.
3. Be concise. State the opinion, give the reason, then get out.
4. Be specific about the football. “He was bad” is less interesting than “he passed up the
   aggressive throw all afternoon.”
5. Admit uncertainty when it is real. That does not weaken the voice; it makes the strong calls
   more credible.
6. Never invent statistics, credentials, sources, hit rates, or a history the site does not have.

### The tone test

Before posting, ask:

- Does this sound like a person, or like a social-media manager writing for a brand?
- Is there an actual opinion?
- Is there a reason for the opinion?
- Is the joke helping the point, or replacing it?

## 4. Content Without Becoming a Negativity Account

Quarterback rankings naturally create criticism. The answer is not to remove negative opinions.
The answer is to make criticism one part of the account instead of the entire identity.

### The content mix

Aim for roughly two constructive, positive, curious, or analytical posts for every pure negative
post. This is a guardrail, not a spreadsheet quota.

The six useful content lanes are:

1. **The List** — the weekly ranking and the most important movement
2. **The Case For** — why a quarterback deserves more credit
3. **The Comparison** — one specific reason QB A is above QB B
4. **The Concern** — what is keeping a quarterback down
5. **The Receipt** — a prediction that held up or a take that did not
6. **The Reply** — a timely reaction under someone else’s post

### Rules for negative posts

- Criticize the performance or decision, not the person.
- Explain what you wanted to see instead.
- Do not turn one bad game into a permanent character judgment.
- Be especially careful with young quarterbacks and injuries.
- If the post is only “this quarterback sucks,” it needs either a sharper football point or a much
  better joke.
- Do not pile on simply because a player is already the day’s target.

### Example

Flat version:

> Drake Maye was terrible. How is he top ten?

Top10QB version:

> Maye is still tenth, but Sunday was the entire case against him: too many plays where the
> athleticism had to rescue a decision that arrived late. He is on notice.

The second version is still critical. It also gives people something real to argue about.

## 5. The Publishing Rhythm

The plan has to fit inside three to five hours per week. A daily posting quota will either fail or
make the account feel manufactured.

### Minimum viable week

**Tuesday — The List**

- Publish the ranking graphic
- Post one short explanation of the biggest disagreement
- Pin the current list
- Spend 15–20 minutes replying to early responses

**Thursday — The Case**

- One original post: a positive case, a head-to-head comparison, or the biggest question in the
  list
- Reply to a few relevant quarterback conversations

**Sunday — Watch and react**

- Focus on one or two games you were going to watch anyway
- Write 5–10 useful replies during meaningful moments
- Make an original post only when the moment connects directly to the list

**Monday — Receipts**

- Save the evidence that will change Tuesday’s list
- When deserved, publish a short “I was wrong” or “the list saw this coming” post

### Healthy weekly range

- 4–7 original posts, including the Tuesday list
- 20–40 thoughtful replies
- One clear positive or “case for” post
- One disagreement or comparison worth debating

These are ranges, not requirements. A quiet NFL week should produce less content than a major
injury, trade, benching, or playoff weekend.

## 6. X Is Distribution, Not the Product

At a small follower count, replies are useful because they put the voice in front of people who do
not know the account yet. The goal is not to reply everywhere. The goal is to add something that
makes a reader curious enough to visit the profile.

### Where to reply

Use a mix of:

- Large national NFL accounts for reach
- Team and beat-reporter accounts for more focused conversations
- Smart mid-sized creators who may actually respond
- Fans already debating a quarterback on the list

Being early can help, but speed is not worth posting something generic.

### The reply test

A good Top10QB reply should do at least one of these:

- Connect the moment to the current list
- Add a specific football observation
- Make a concise comparison
- Deliver a joke that still makes sense to someone who has never seen the account

“What a throw” is usually not enough.

### X Premium

Treat Premium as a 30-day experiment, not a strategic requirement. X changes its product and
distribution frequently, and no outside estimate should be treated as a permanent algorithm rule.

If you try it, compare:

- Reply impressions
- Profile visits
- New followers after high-performing replies
- Site clicks from X

Keep it only if the account clearly gets enough value to justify the cost.

## 7. The Website’s Job

The site is not a second social network. It has four jobs:

1. Explain the current list quickly
2. Prove that old lists have been kept
3. Show the reasoning behind each rank
4. Convert a curious visitor into a follower or future email reader

### The public pages

- **The List:** the current top ten and the main take for each quarterback
- **Archive:** every list that has actually been published
- **I Was Wrong:** real misses, added when there is enough evidence to revisit the call
- **About:** the ranking definition and the one-person premise

### The graphics tool

The private admin now creates:

- A wide ranking graphic for X and link previews
- A portrait ranking graphic
- A No.1 quarterback card

These should remain code-built because names, ranks, arrows, and dates have to be exact. Canva can
still be used for a special one-off design, but it should not be required for the weekly workflow.

AI image generation is best used for supporting material such as an original background texture.
It should not generate fake action photos, team logos, player likenesses, or the ranking text.

### The site freeze

After the core flow is stable, stop redesigning during the season. Fix broken or confusing things,
but keep design experiments off the weekly critical path.

## 8. Email: Make Only the Promise You Can Keep

The current form should be treated as an early-access list until an email service is connected.
Do not promise “every Tuesday at 9 a.m.” while the site only stores addresses.

Launch the actual email edition when all three are true:

1. A sending service is connected
2. A test email has reached your own inbox correctly
3. You can send the same short format every week without adding more than 20 minutes of work

The first version can be extremely small:

- The ten names
- Two sentences on the biggest move
- A link to the full list

The email should reuse the work already done for the site. It should not become another essay that
has to be written every Tuesday.

## 9. AI Content System: Codex or Claude

Codex can do the same category of preparation described in the earlier Claude Cowork plan:
generate drafts, prepare a game-day brief, build and maintain graphic templates, inspect the site,
and turn structured ranking data into a weekly content packet.

The more important choice is not the model. It is the workflow.

### Recommended division of labor

**You decide:**

- The ranking
- The real reason for every controversial placement
- Which jokes sound like you
- What gets posted

**AI prepares:**

- Three possible Tuesday captions
- A short thread draft
- The biggest riser/faller post
- Two positive “case for” ideas
- One head-to-head comparison
- A Sunday watch list tied to the current rankings
- Candidates for “I Was Wrong,” based only on real saved takes

**The site produces:**

- Exact weekly graphics from the published data
- The archive
- The permanent links

### Codex vs. Claude

- Use **Codex** when the task touches the website, admin, database, graphics code, or files in this
  project. It can see the same source that publishes the list, which reduces copying and mistakes.
- Use **either Codex or Claude** for brainstorming and writing drafts. Pick the one whose drafts
  require less editing.
- Do not keep separate “truth” in two tools. The published list and saved takes should live in the
  Top10QB admin/database. AI drafts are disposable.

There is no need to choose one tool forever. The graphics system also removes the highest-risk
part of that choice: neither model needs to redraw ten names correctly every Tuesday.

### Voice training

Build a short voice file from real posts, not invented catchphrases. After 30–50 posts, save:

- Five posts that sound exactly right
- Five that feel too mean
- Five that feel too polished or corporate
- Words and bits you naturally repeat
- Phrases the AI should never use

Update it monthly. Real examples will teach the voice better than a long fictional Rankmaster
backstory.

### Automation rule

Nothing posts automatically.

Automation may collect the ranking, create graphics, and prepare drafts. You review and post every
public word. The account’s advantage is taste; removing your approval would remove the advantage.

## 10. Measurement

Follower count matters, but it is a result, not a useful weekly instruction.

Track these once per week:

- Original posts published
- Thoughtful replies published
- Total impressions
- Profile visits
- New followers
- Site visits from X
- Email signups

Use a four-week average so one viral post or one quiet week does not control the strategy.

Also keep a small qualitative log:

- Which topic produced the best conversation?
- Which post sounded most like you?
- Which post felt too negative?
- Which reply led to profile visits or a follow?

### Decision rules

- **Impressions but few profile visits:** the post worked, but the brand connection was weak.
- **Profile visits but few follows:** improve the pinned post, bio, or clarity of the premise.
- **Follows but few site visits:** not automatically a problem; X may be doing its job.
- **Site visits but few email signups:** the email offer is unclear or not yet valuable.
- **Posting feels exhausting:** reduce frequency before outsourcing the voice.

Five thousand followers by the Super Bowl can remain a stretch outcome. It should not be the
number used to judge whether the season was worth doing.

## 11. Season 1 Priorities

In order:

1. Publish a real list consistently
2. Develop a recognizable human voice
3. Build the habit of useful replies
4. Keep the archive honest
5. Learn which topics and formats earn profile visits
6. Start the email edition only when it can be sent reliably
7. Think about money later

Sponsorships, affiliate links, merchandise, and paid newsletters are distractions until the
account has a real audience that returns for the list.

## 12. Now Through Week 1

### Phase 1 — Finish the foundation

- Confirm the one-sentence ranking rule
- Publish the revised About page
- Test the admin publishing flow on a phone
- Download all three weekly graphic formats
- Make sure social links show a real preview image
- Start saving real posts for the voice file

### Phase 2 — Establish the rhythm

- Publish an offseason list
- Follow the minimum viable week for two weeks
- Track how long the entire process takes
- Reduce any step that turns the list into a production job
- Add the first “I Was Wrong” entry only when there is a real, specific call to revisit

### Phase 3 — Rehearse

- Run two complete Tuesday rehearsals:
  1. Enter the ranking
  2. Publish it
  3. Download the graphics
  4. Prepare the caption
  5. Check the social preview
  6. Post manually
- Create one Sunday brief with AI and decide whether it actually helps
- Keep Canva as an optional tool, not a dependency

### Week 1

- Publish the list
- Pin it
- Spend time in the replies
- Do not redesign the site

## 13. The Short Version

Make one good list. Explain the disagreements. Talk to football fans like a person. Be willing to
be wrong. Use AI to remove production work, not to manufacture opinions. Keep the joke, but let
the human voice lead.
